Access to opportunities cannot really be captured by a single metric: the reasons we go to a supermarket are not the same for which we commute to work, or go to the stadium. Proximity access, reaching amenities that are useful for everyday life, is one dimension of our needs; Opportunity access, getting to opportunities that can be further from our home, is another dimension. In Close & Connected, we map the two concepts and put them in the same framework.

A vibrant neighbourhood that does not have a metro station or a reliable tram or bus lane is a **place** where we can live **close** to people and opportunities; a residential area near a connected train station is a **node** where we can live **connected** to the rest of the city. The node-place model (Bertolini, 1996) is almost thirty years old but very contemporary too: with proper planning, node & places can coincide, as residential density is a driver of both, and neighbourhoods can be **close & connected**.

Using a **proximity score** that maps what we can reach by walking, and an **opportunity score** that maps what we can reach by public transport, we have the tools to assess how close & connected is an area. In our results, we find correlated metrics: dense areas can have proximity services but also sustain good public transport. But the two differ in many cases: when suburban areas have good public transport (spatially integrated but socially isolated) or when peripheral areas are lively neighbourhoods but bad public transport options (socially integrated but spatially isolated).

![Paris, the four Close & Connected zones, cell by cell](close-and-connected/hero.webp) {wide}

The layer puts the Atlas’s two dimensions side by side. **Proximity** is what is close by on foot, the dimension of the 15-minute city. **Opportunity** is what public transport connects you to across the city, the dimension of CityChrone. Both are scored on the same cells by the P.O.V. framework: how many services a person reaches by walking in a short time, and by public transport in a longer one.

## Four zones

Split at the city’s medians, every cell falls in one of four zones. Close, Connected or Isolated?

![The four zones: Close & Connected, Close, Connected and Total isolation](close-and-connected/quadrants.webp "The medians are weighted by residents, not taken over cells: in Rome, proximity 2,591 and opportunity 9,435. The paper’s names were inclusion, spatial isolation, social isolation and total isolation.")

The four are not a ranking, but a photography of what is missing. A place that is Close needs transit; a place that is Connected needs shops, schools and services where today there is only a station. One score for both would prescribe the same cure to each.

## Both scores at once

Ranking the city’s residents along the two scores, the two ranks mix into one colour scale: light blue where a place has more close by than most of the city, pink where transit connects it to more, deep indigo where it has both, pale where it has little of either.

## Cells or residents?

In Rome 83.0% of cells are in total isolation, but only 46.1% of residents live in them: isolated cells are large and thinly settled. In New York the figures are 54.1% of cells and 41.0% of residents, in Paris 50.3% and 42.5%. The cartogram helps giving the right weight to each cell.
